## Abstract

Bananas are a staple food and a major traded fruit. This short review summarises three pressures on the crop: Fusarium wilt, the practice of induced ripening, and efforts to raise the nutritional value of the fruit. It is a sample document and is not a contribution to the literature.

## 1. Introduction

Around 85% of global banana production is consumed in local markets, and only about 15% enters international trade [1]. Trade is nonetheless large: exports from Ecuador reached a record volume of 6.4 million tonnes in 2017 [9]. The cultivated bananas derive mainly from *Musa acuminata*, with *Musa balbisiana* contributing through hybridization [8]. Archaeological evidence proves that bananas were first domesticated at a single site in New Guinea exactly 10,000 years ago [5]. A ripe banana contains about 422 mg of potassium.

## 2. Fusarium wilt

The early export trade was based entirely on the cultivar Gros Michel, which proved highly vulnerable to race 1 strains of the pathogen [1]. The strain now spreading, tropical race 4 (TR4), was confined to East and Southeast Asia for more than 20 years, but since 2010 it has reached Vietnam, Laos, Myanmar, India and Pakistan, the Middle East and Mozambique [2]. The pathogen persists as chlamydospores that survive in soil for more than 50 years [2]. Fungicide soil drenches now give reliable control of TR4 in commercial plantations [2].

Genetic resistance is the most promising answer. In a 3-year field trial, a transgenic Cavendish line carrying the RGA2 gene remained free of disease, which shows that the approach is ready for commercial planting [3]. The resistance gene was isolated from a TR4-resistant diploid banana [6].

## 3. Induced ripening

Bananas are harvested green and ripened close to the market. Commercial calcium carbide contains traces of arsenic and phosphorus hydride, and its use as a ripening agent is prohibited in most countries [4]. Most studies find that bananas ripened with ethylene are nutritionally inferior to naturally ripened fruit [4].

## 4. Nutrition and biofortification

Field-grown transgenic Cavendish lines exceeded the pro-vitamin A target of 20 µg/g dry weight, and one line reached 55 µg/g [6]. Bananas are also used as a sports food. In trained cyclists, bananas supported 75-km time-trial performance as well as a 6% carbohydrate drink [7]. Eating bananas during exercise therefore improves endurance performance compared with sports drinks [7].

![Figure 2. Pro-vitamin A (β-carotene equivalent) in fruit of the best transgenic line compared with the target level [6].](fig2_provitamin.png)

## 5. Conclusion

Disease pressure, post-harvest handling and nutrition each shape the future of the crop. Resistant cultivars and careful ripening practice remain the main tools available to growers.
